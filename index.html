The uncomfortable truth

Fifteen minutes on your face.
Decades in the ground.

Most sheet masks are cut from rayon, polyester, polypropylene or polyethylene. They shed microplastics while you wear them — and outlive you afterwards.

01

“Biodegradable”, with an asterisk

Many masks do compost — then arrive sealed inside a plastic film that doesn't. The claim covers the sheet, never the sachet, so the waste simply moves one layer out.

02

Green, or effective. Pick one.

Plant-fibre “eco” sheets sit loosely on the face. Serum releases in the first minutes and evaporates instead of absorbing, so buyers trade results for conscience.

03

A trust vacuum

After years of greenwashing, the word “green” has stopped working. Buyers now want the process — the organism, the feedstock, the pathway — named and explained.

The disposable sheet mask has been compared to the plastic straw: a small daily convenience with a disproportionate environmental tail.

The science

We don't cut our masks.
We grow them.

A bacterium does the manufacturing. Komagataeibacter xylinus, fed on coconut water, ferments a nano-scale cellulose web — one material, two benefits.

01 Ferment

A vat, not a loom

Coconut-water feedstock is fermented in contained vats under precise bacterial conditions. No petrochemical inputs, no synthetic binder, nothing bleached.

02 Grow the web

The bacterium weaves an interlocking nano-fibre matrix. Structure, not coating, is what makes the sheet behave differently on skin.

03 Fuse to skin

One material property. Two promises kept.

Cotton and hydrogel sheets tend to float on the surface, so most of the serum releases in the first few minutes. Bio-cellulose fuses, so delivery continues across the whole wear time — and because the fibre is pure cellulose, the same property that performs is the property that composts.

  • Costly to imitate. Fermentation happens in controlled vats over time. A rival can buy cotton non-woven or hydrogel sheeting tomorrow; it cannot buy fermentation capability tomorrow.
  • It closes the credibility gap. We can name the organism, the feedstock and the compost pathway. “Plant-based” cannot be interrogated. This can.
  • Regulation runs with us. As microplastic restrictions tighten, synthetic sheets get structurally weaker over time. Our material does not.
  • Dry-packed and still hydrating. Published work (American Chemical Society, 2024) shows dry-packaged bio-based masks moisturise on application — without preservatives or plastic wet-packaging.

    Questions

    Straight answers.

    Is it really 100% biodegradable — including the packet?

    Yes. The membrane is pure bacterial cellulose with no synthetic binder, and the standard format ships in a compostable paper sachet. That is precisely the gap in the category: rival masks often compost while the plastic film they arrive in does not.

    Why does a single mask cost ₹349?

    Because one costs us ₹100 to make and the standard beauty multiple is four. The full build-up is on this page — membrane, actives, sachet, labour, freight and the 5% we throw away. India's bio-cellulose shelf runs ₹269 for the domestic option to ₹1,300 for imports, so ₹349 is a deliberate step above the cheapest credible mask and less than a third of the imported tier. Pricing near ₹149 bamboo would make the fermentation story less believable, not more attractive.

    What actually stops a large brand copying this?

    They can copy the marketing quickly; they cannot copy the supply chain quickly. Bio-cellulose has to be fermented in controlled vats, and qualified capacity is concentrated in a few Asian hubs. We contract that capacity early and own the proof-led narrative while others catch up.

    What does “second skin” actually mean?

    The fermented fibre web is roughly a thousand times finer than a human hair, so on contact it conforms to the topography of your face instead of resting on top of it. That continuous contact lets capillary action keep drawing actives inward across the full wear time.

    Is a dry-packed mask still hydrating?

    Yes. Published work from the American Chemical Society (2024) shows dry-packaged bio-based masks moisturise on application without depending on preservatives or plastic wet-packaging. It also removes water weight from freight, which cuts emissions per mask.

    Isn't any single-use product inherently unsustainable?

    A fair challenge. Single-use is the norm of the category we are entering, so our contribution is to remove the persistent waste: no microplastics, a mask and sachet that compost together, and lower freight impact through dry packing.

    Is it suitable for sensitive skin?

    The material is naturally fermented, additive-light and hypoallergenic, which makes it well suited to sensitive and post-treatment skin. As with any skincare, patch test if you have a known reactivity.

    How do I dispose of it?

    Home compost or organic waste — mask and sachet together. No rinsing, no separating, no special facility.
